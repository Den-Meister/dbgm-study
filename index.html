Block A

Strategy & International Business Foundations

What "international business" and "strategy" mean, how strategy differs from tactics, the author definitions, and the frameworks for describing and locating strategy.

Source: 11 Aug deck

Core definitions (memorize the shape, restate it in plain words)

International — relating to or affecting two or more nations.
Business — an occupation, profession, or trade; the purchase and sale of goods/services to make a profit; a profit-seeking enterprise.
International business — any business that operates across international borders. At its most basic, the sale of goods and services between countries — but other forms also exist.
Corporate strategy — a unique, long-term plan/framework designed to gain competitive advantage over other market participants while delivering on customer/client and stakeholder promises. Guides decisions like hiring, new products, entering markets.
International business strategy — plans guiding commercial transactions between entities in different countries; the plans/policies of private companies (not governments) to achieve an overall goal — e.g. increased profits.

Strategy vs. Tactics

Strategy
The overall plan

The means by which people or organizations achieve their objectives; the overall plan for deploying resources to establish a favorable position. Long-term. (Military root: the science and art of command applied to the overall conduct of warfare.)

Tactic
The specific maneuver

A maneuver or action calculated to achieve some end; a scheme for a specific maneuver. Near-future.

📌 The 3 manager types (his analogy)

Creatives — "Let's go to Shanghai." Decision-makers — "OK, let's go tomorrow." Executives — which transport, the route, the timing. (Idea → decision → execution.)

Characteristics of strategic decisions

They are important, involve a significant commitment of resources, and are not easily reversible.

Strategy defined by authors — each name paired with its one idea

Porter (2000)
Unique position

Strategy is the creation of a unique and valuable position, involving a different set of activities.

Cite for: strategy = choosing a different set of activities, not just doing the same ones better.

Collis & Rustag (2008)
State it simply

Most executives can't articulate the objective, scope, and advantage of their business in a simple statement — and if they can't, neither can anyone else.

Cite for: the test of a real strategy is whether it fits one clear sentence.

Grant (2010)
Practice-driven

Business strategy's evolution is driven more by the practical needs of business than by theory. In the 1950s–60s, execs struggled to coordinate decisions and keep control in ever larger, more complex firms.

Cite for: strategy theory follows business practice — it grew from managers' real coordination problems.

Dess, McNamara, Eiser & Lee (2021)

Block B

International Trade

The models that explain why countries trade, the empirical patterns, and the actors and legal framework of international trade.

Source: 18 Aug deck

International trade — foreign trade: the exchange of goods, services, capital and intellectual/personal rights between individuals of different states. Two senses: (1) turnover of goods between countries; (2) also turnover of services, capital, labor and information.
Trade pattern — the composition of a country's imports and exports, and the volume of its trade with the rest of the world, which is likely to change over time.
Trade pattern theories — two theories explain patterns of trade: comparative advantage and increasing returns to scale.

Framework — the models of international trade (the spine of this block)

ModelAuthor(s)Core idea
Absolute advantageAdam Smith (1776)
Wealth of Nations		Reply to mercantilism (grow wealth by pushing exports, blocking imports). Unfettered trade benefits both importer and exporter — each produces what it makes best.
Comparative advantageRicardoTwo-country, two-good example: countries gain even if one has an absolute advantage in both. Specialize in the good with the lowest opportunity cost and export it. Predicts specialization & direction of trade.
Heckscher-OhlinHeckscher & OhlinBest-known alternative to Ricardo. Advantage comes from differing factor endowments and how intensively sectors use them. H-O theorem: a country exports the good using intensively the factor it has in relative abundance.
New trade theoryKrugman (1979)Most world trade is between similar developed countries and much is intra-industry (two-way, same industry). Monopolistic firms each make a variety + economies of scale; consumers have a "love for variety." Countries trade varieties even with no comparative advantage.
Recent advancesMelitz (2003)Micro-foundations: firms are heterogeneous in productive efficiency; models which firms export, affecting industry dynamics, aggregate productivity, and wages.

Use in an answer: name the author + the one distinguishing idea; if asked which model fits a given case, apply the rule in the box below.

⚠️ Which model for which trade?

New trade theory best explains trade between similar developed countries and intra-industry trade (most of world trade). Comparative-advantage models best explain developed ↔ less-developed trade. The gravity equation can be derived from both.

Empirical patterns — Blonigen & Wilson (2012), 4 findings

1 · Phenomenal growth

Trade is growing at a phenomenal rate.

2 · Few dominant players

Trade is dominated by relatively few countries, who tend to stay dominant.

3 · Uneven but stable

Growth rates differ a lot across products, yet relative product-market sizes stay stable.

4 · Shift to Asia

Over 40 years, major flows shifted from US–Europe to Asia–US.

📌 Globalization to the consumer

More choices, generally lower prices (not always), and a blurred national identity of products: a Volvo owned by China's Geely, a Jaguar owned by India's Tata, a Dodge from Italy's Fiat, foreign brands built in the US. The point: brands and production are decoupled across borders.

Actors of international trade (the legal-framework slide)

States

Key, indisputable creators of international law. Most are both de jure (in law) and de facto (in reality). Developed vs. developing take different approaches.

Corporations

Often multinationals; profit-driven; lobby states & international organizations to protect their interests — with the same accountability questions raised about NGOs.

International organizations

Intergovernmental orgs / NGOs formed between governments; decide by one-state-one-vote, consensus, or weighted voting.

Regional organizations

Regional bodies coordinating trade among neighboring states.

Juridical & physical persons

Individuals increasingly hold rights and responsibilities under international law (e.g. human-rights law).

Arbitration associations

Handle dispute settlement — often preferred because foreign arbitral awards enjoy a single, near-universal enforcement regime.

📌 International commercial contracts

A contract is "international" when two or more states conclude an agreement (e.g. the Vienna 1980 UN Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods; the Hague Principles 2015). Parties may choose the governing law; if they don't, a court applies conflict-of-laws rules. Arbitration is common because foreign arbitral decisions have an almost inclusive enforcement regime.

Block C

Free Trade, Trade Blocs & Protectionism

The levels of economic integration, why free trade helps (and hurts), the major blocs, and the toolkit of protectionism.

Source: 25 Aug deck + "Trade Blocs" class video

The integration ladder — the full spectrum of trade blocs

A trade bloc is a group of countries that agree to reduce trade barriers among themselves. They differ along two axes: internal rules (how far barriers between members fall) and external rules (how members treat non-members). Read it as a ladder — each rung keeps everything below it and adds one new thing. Naming what each rung adds answers any "distinguish them" question.

1 · Preferential Trade Area
Lower, don't eliminate

Members lower but do not eliminate barriers among themselves. Non-members aren't addressed — each keeps its own policy toward them. The lowest level of commitment.

2 · Free Trade Area
Eliminate internal barriers

Members eliminate barriers among themselves but keep independent external policies. Ex: NAFTA (Canada, Mexico, US).

3 · Customs Union
+ common external barriers

Eliminate internal barriers and adopt common external barriers toward non-members. Ex: Russia, Kazakhstan & Belarus (customs union, 2010).

4 · Common Market
+ resources move freely

Everything above plus free movement of resources (e.g. labor) among members. Ex: Mercosur (Southern Cone Market); East/West African Common Markets.

5 · Economic Union
+ one economic policy

Everything above plus a uniform set of economic policies; a common currency forces a common monetary policy. Ex: the European Union once the euro was adopted.

6 · Full integration
One state

Complete integration of the member states into a single unified economy. Ex: the United States.

⚠️ How to answer "distinguish them"

Walk up the ladder and name what each rung adds: lower barriers (PTA) → eliminate them (FTA) → common external wall (Customs Union) → free movement of resources (Common Market) → uniform economic policy/currency (Economic Union) → one state (full integration). The single most-tested jump is the common external tariff (FTA → Customs Union).

🌍 Two naming systems in the course materials — don't blur them

The sources label these stages two different ways, and either can appear on the exam. Keep them straight:

Free trade — policies permitting inexpensive imports and exports without tariffs or other trade barriers. A free trade agreement (FTA) is a group of countries agreeing to lower tariffs/barriers to trade more with each other.

An FTA reaches further than "no tariffs"

The point to make in an answer: a modern FTA doesn't stop at the tariff line — it reaches behind the border. It reduces/eliminates tariffs, tackles behind-the-border barriers, encourages investment, tightens rules like intellectual property, and opens up e-commerce and government procurement. So "free trade agreement" means deeper economic rule-setting, not just cheaper imports.

Benefits of free trade — Pettinger (2019)

1 · Comparative advantage

Specialize where opportunity cost is lower → higher welfare for all.

2 · Trade creation

Lower tariffs shift consumption from high-cost to low-cost producers.

3 · Increased exports

Lower foreign tariffs → more exports → jobs and growth.

4 · Economies of scale

Specializing → larger scale → lower average costs → lower prices.

5 · Increased competition

More rivals → cut costs, more efficiency, curbs domestic monopolies.

6 · Engine of growth

World trade has grown ~7% a year since 1945.

7 · Use of surplus raw materials

Qatar's oil is only valuable with trade; resource-poor Japan needs trade to avoid low GDP.

8 · Tariffs breed inefficiency

Protected industries have little incentive to cut costs.

⚠️ Free trade — the downsides he lists

Harmful to the environment · bad working conditions · intellectual-property theft · job outsourcing · degradation of natural resources. (PROS/CONS framing: PROS — growth, lower government spending, technology transfer; CONS — outsourcing, poor working conditions, resource degradation.)

RCEP ≈ 30.5%USMCA ≈ 28%EU ≈ 17.9%AfCFTA ≈ 2.9%Mercosur ≈ 2.4%

📌 RCEP — the headline bloc

The Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership: a free trade agreement among 15 Asia-Pacific nations, officially the biggest trading bloc in history — ~30% of global GDP and population, larger than USMCA and the EU. India pulled out. It sets rules on investment, competition, telecommunications, intellectual property, and e-commerce.

Framework — the protectionist toolkit

Tool 1 · Tariffs

Charged to the importing country; raise the price of imports. Three types: Scientific (item-by-item), Peril-point (industry-specific harm threshold), Retaliatory (response to partners' excessive duties).

Tool 2 · Import quotas

Non-tariff barrier limiting the quantity imported over a period. Can prevent dumping. An embargo (total ban) is the most severe quota.

Tool 3 · Product standards

Safety/quality rules that block imports failing internal controls. Example: French raw-milk cheeses aged under 60 days are banned in the US — protecting US producers.

Tool 4 · Government subsidies

Direct (cash payments) or indirect (interest-free loans, tax breaks). Export subsidies push domestic firms to expand abroad.

Dumping — when foreign firms sell products at artificially low prices (below production cost) in a market, often due to unfair subsidies or overproduction.

Protectionism — the balance sheet

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Protect industry & jobs · protect the consumer · retaliation against unfair competition · national security · better balance of trade · shields emerging domestic industries. Lack of economic efficiency · less consumer choice · retaliation risk · higher prices → inflation, lower real incomes · lower productivity & innovation. Broad economist consensus: protectionism harms growth/welfare; free trade helps.
📌 Real-world examples

EU Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) tariffs protecting farmers · US tariffs on Chinese tyres · the Banana Wars. Protectionist-measure counts: China 200–300, the US over 800, UK & Germany ~300 each; most measures target China (a "new trade war"). Per Credit Suisse / Global Trade Alert, the most protectionist country is the US.

Strategies for Locating Production Facilities

Where should an international firm put its production to minimize cost and improve quality? Beyond country-level factors, weigh technological factors and product factors — then choose to concentrate or decentralize.

Source: the course video "Strategies for Locating Production Facilities," shown in class.

Technological factors — 3 traits of a manufacturing technology

1 · Fixed costs
How costly to set up a plant

High fixed costs → serve the world from one / few locations (e.g. a $5B semiconductor plant). Low → produce in many locations: local responsiveness + a hedge against adverse currency moves.

2 · Minimum efficient scale
Where scale economies run out

The output level at which most plant-level scale economies are exhausted. Large MES vs global demand → centralize. Low MES → several plants (smartphones: MES ≈ 5M/yr vs demand >1B/yr → Apple, Samsung, Huawei assemble in many sites).

3 · Flexible manufacturing system
Adapts to type & quantity

Machines/computers reconfigure to make varied products and enable make-to-order customization; raises efficiency and lowers cost — but higher upfront equipment cost.

Product factors — 2 features of the product itself

1 · Value-to-weight ratio
Monetary value per kg

High (electronics, pharmaceuticals) → transport is a tiny % of cost → concentrate in the optimal location and serve the world. Low (refined sugar, bulk chemicals, paint, petroleum) → transport is a big % → produce near major markets.

2 · Universal needs
Same tastes worldwide?

Universal (industrial electronics, steel, bulk chemicals; calculators, PCs, game consoles) → little need for local responsiveness → concentrate. Not universal (tastes differ by nation) → decentralize.

🌍 Big idea

Crossing a border adds one thing above all — foreign-currency exposure — plus a different political, legal, tax and cultural environment. Everything in this block either measures that exposure, pays across it, funds through it, or is an institution that keeps it stable.

Bretton Woods — the system behind it all

Exchange risk — the three types (plus political risk)

Currency risk

Day-to-day exchange-rate swings on contracts to buy/sell at fixed prices in the short run.

⧉ Picture it: the price tag moves between signing and getting paid — e.g. an exporter invoiced USD 1M due in 90 days, and the dollar falls first.

Business risk

Long-run moves from unanticipated changes in relative economic conditions.

⧉ Picture it: the climate changes, not just the weather — a lasting shift makes a whole foreign operation uncompetitive.

Translation risk

Foreign income must be translated back to USD for accounting even if not actually converted.

⧉ Picture it: same score, smaller scoreboard — local profits unchanged, but they look smaller in the parent's books.

Financial derivatives — the tools against exchange risk

Financial derivative — an instrument linked to a specific underlying asset (another financial instrument or a good); its price depends on the market price of that underlying asset.
Forward

The counterparties agree to exchange, on a specified date, a specified quantity of an underlying item (financial or real asset) at an agreed contract price. Execution is mandatory at the end of the specified period.

⧉ Remember it as: booking today's price for a later delivery.

Future

An agreement between seller and buyer for the seller to deliver a specific quantity and grade of an identified commodity at a fixed time in the future, at a price agreed when the contract is first entered into.

⧉ Remember it as: a forward with standard terms.

Option

The buyer pays the seller for the right but not the obligation to buy or sell a specified item at an agreed price in a specified period. The buyer can also sell the option contract itself.

⧉ Remember it as: insurance — pay a fee, use it only if it helps.

Swap

A spot purchase (sale) of an asset combined with a forward sale (purchase) of it — e.g. buy a currency in the spot market and agree to sell it back at a price set in advance. Types: interest-rate swaps and currency swaps.

⧉ Remember it as: swap now, swap back later.

⧉ Outside — how they are used (the deck defines them but doesn't spell this out)

Hedging: a firm expecting foreign currency later can sell it forward today to lock the rate (removes the downside and the upside); an option protects against a fall while keeping the upside, for a fee (the premium). Futures are usually standardised and exchange-traded, forwards private and tailored. Each contract covers a set amount and period — protection beyond that needs more contracts.

Capital-market instruments (the depository receipts & bonds)

GDR — Global Depository Receipt

⧉ A depository receipt is like a coat-check ticket for shares a bank holds.

Shares issued in the home currency, lodged with a depository bank that issues receipts (in USD, or euros) — a bank certificate issued in more than one country, listed on foreign exchanges, freely negotiable. Holder gets dividends and bonuses but no voting rights; convertible to shares after 45 days. Ex: ICICI, Wipro, Reliance, Infosys raise foreign exchange through GDRs.

ADR — American Depository Receipt

Like a GDR but issued by a US depository bank, only to US residents, traded only in the US capital market; prices and dividends in USD, traded like US shares. Lets US investors hold overseas companies. Types: sponsored / unsponsored. Banks named: JPMorgan, Citibank, Deutsche Bank, BNY Mellon.

IDR — Indian Depository Receipt

The Indian version of a GDR: an Indian depository, denominated in rupees, available only in Indian markets (Indian residents can bid too). First foreign issuer: Standard Chartered.

FCCB — Foreign Currency Convertible Bond

Debt + equity hybrid: a bond (always bought/sold in foreign markets, lower fixed interest than non-convertible debt) convertible into shares or depository receipts after a stipulated time; full face value redeemed at maturity, ~5 years; dilutes ownership and earnings per share; holders can't control the conversion rate.

Choosing a source: no avenue is perfect, so firms often form a portfolio of two or more. Factors that decide: purpose and duration of the business, risk-taking capacity, tax benefits, the financial strength of the investor.

⚠️ ADR vs GDR

ADR = United States only. GDR = issued across (mainly European) markets / multiple countries. Both are negotiable certificates a depository bank issues against foreign shares; both help acquire resources & invest across borders.

The Bretton Woods institutions

IMF — International Monetary Fund

Promotes global economic growth & financial stability, coordinates currency exchange and the balance of payments, reduces poverty. Monitors the world's currencies and encourages trade. Voting is quota-weighted by economic indicators (GDP, economic liberalism, official reserves) — not the UN's one-country-one-vote.

World Bank

A cooperative of 189 member countries; provides development loans to developing countries to reduce poverty. Focus: long-term development (IMF = macro/financial stability).

Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)

FDI — cross-border investment where an investor in one economy establishes a lasting interest in, and a significant degree of influence over, an enterprise in another economy.
4 types

Horizontal (same industry abroad — most common; e.g. a US cellphone provider buying a chain of phone stores in China), Vertical (a complementary business — e.g. a US manufacturer acquiring an interest in its foreign raw-material supplier), Conglomerate and Platform (named in the deck without definitions; ⧉ usually: an unrelated business abroad; a base abroad to export to a third market).

Why it matters

Primary source of external capital and increased revenue; boosts growth and development, eases trade, creates jobs, develops human capital, transfers skilled resources, can bring tax incentives. The US was the largest recipient of FDI in 2019 and 2020.

Overseas / global expansion — a company's strategic decision to extend operations into foreign markets by establishing a physical or operational presence (sales offices, factories, service centres) to reach new customers, tap global talent and gain a competitive edge.

Why firms expand — and when they should

Why expand

Increased revenue (new segments) · cost reduction (cheaper production/labour) · risk mitigation (diversify beyond one market) · competitive pressure (keep pace) · access to resources/talent.

The 3 elements that must align

1. Traction — a signal of market potential. 2. Feasible profitability / interest. 3. Ability to invest in the right level of localization. "A signal to expand doesn't mean a firm should — weigh interest and capacity."

📌 Considerations before entering

Cultural sensitivity (respect differences — ties to Block F) · legal compliance (navigate local regulation) · resource allocation (finance, people, tech) · adaptability (flex the strategy as needed).

Disadvantages & risks of expanding

Set-up costs

Often must form a local subsidiary/entity — lawyers, registration, and minimum capital (e.g. €25,000 in Germany).

Permanent-establishment tax risk

A fixed place of business abroad can trigger corporate income tax there — an unexpected tax bill.

Insufficient market understanding

Without first-hand knowledge a firm can misjudge demand — Tesco and Starbucks are cited expansion failures.

Non-compliance

"You don't know what you don't know" — wrong tax/payroll/employment handling → fines and back-charges.

Internationalization Theory & the M&A Evaluation Model

Source: September deck (Moskovicz 2019 paper + theory slides)

Prof. Moskovicz's M&A country-evaluation model

📌 "A Worldwide Business Evaluation Model for M&A" (Moskovicz, 2019)

His own paper. The idea: no single variable predicts whether an M&A (or market entry) will succeed, so the evaluator should list all relevant variables, rate each, and weight them by relevance. For a country, score each variable Low / Med / High and Positive / Neutral / Negative, times a Relevance weight, and total it (the "National General Evaluation", done separately by sector).

Variables include: clear/stable commercial rules · technological development · communications · infrastructure · justice-court independence · political stability · macroeconomic stability · corruption level · protective laws / import barriers · unemployment · local-currency stability · rigid labour rules · investment trend · incentives for foreign investment · free access to FX · dividend taxation/limits for foreign firms.

⚠️ Why a weighted model (the authors)

Haleblian (2009): the causes of M&A failure are under-researched and need more study. King (2004): none of the strategic or financial variables alone predicts variance in post-acquisition performance. Conclusion: a holistic, relevance-weighted assessment is needed — not one magic metric.

⧉ Class-sourced — the model as taught in class (classmate guide)

The guide presents the model as 10 key national variables: clear & stable commercial rules · technological development · connectivity · infrastructure · judicial independence · national political stability · local corruption level · macroeconomic stability · local currency stability · labour flexibility & market conditions — the same ground as the deck's list above. It adds that low corruption is mandatory for public listed companies bound by compliance rules. (The guide's 3-tier extension — host country → target firm → post-merger, scored +10/−10 — is not in the professor's model; treat it as a classmate's elaboration.)

📌 Class case — Chile vs Peru ⧉ figures unverified

Chile was once the top Latin American FDI destination (score cited above 80%); it fell (below 60%) as currency volatility, the estallido social and constitutional process, shifting rules, unemployment and labour rigidity hit several variables at once. Peru rose (above 75%) despite cycling through many presidents, because central-bank autonomy, macro stability and investor protections — free capital repatriation, tax treatment until the investment is recovered — stayed insulated from politics. Lesson: attractiveness moves across the whole variable list; political noise ≠ financial instability.

📌 Class case — the London Boardroom ⧉ class-sourced

An expansion team found a highly profitable target in Argentina, but the parent's board in London — a company listed on the London Stock Exchange — rejected the deal: adding Argentine sovereign risk (default, inflation, capital controls) would make investors punish the share price. Lesson: a public listed firm weighs share-price risk over local profit; a private firm tolerates more risk for returns.

⧉ The guide links Chile's past attractiveness to its "Chapter 14" framework (Capítulo XIV — free repatriation of foreign capital, favourable tax treatment until the investment was recovered) and says Peru adopted similar guarantees. The idea is plausible class content; the legal details are unverified.

How internationalization reshapes strategy

The Firm — resources & capabilitiesThe Industry — environment
National resource availability (the slide's theory of competitive advantage); the impact of national context on how resources develop — Porter's national diamond. A wider market with more diverse customer preferences; more competitors; lower entry barriers; increased buyer power.

Point: internationalization sits between firm and industry — it widens opportunity and intensifies competition.

The two limits every cross-border manager hits

Bounded rationality (Simon, 1982)

"Scarcity of mind": a decision-maker never has complete information (especially about the future) and can't fully process what is available. For an IB strategist it bites in entry-mode choice and in the home-vs-host perspective gap — head office and subsidiary managers deem different information relevant (Verbeke & Yuan, 2005).

Bounded reliability (Verbeke, 2009)

Not about knowing but about doing: actors fail to fully deliver on promises. Sources (Kano & Verbeke, 2015): opportunism (self-interest, false promises) and benevolent preference reversal (good-faith promises abandoned as preferences change — e.g. subsidiary managers chasing local opportunities). Firms add safeguards/enforcement to detect and punish it.

📌 FSAs — firm-specific advantages (Verbeke, 2009)

A firm builds on the broad location advantages (LAs) of its home country → a smaller subset of location-bound (LB) FSAs → a still-smaller subset of non-location-bound (NLB) FSAs that can transfer across borders. Bounded rationality & reliability limit how well those NLB advantages transfer to the host country. Related: SSAs (subsidiary-specific) and ASAs (alliance-specific) advantages.

Types of FSAs and the entry-mode × FSA matrix (Verbeke)

FSAs come as stand-alone FSAs, routines and recombination capabilities. The entry mode a firm picks depends on which FSAs it targets abroad:

Writing the business plan (the deck's guidelines)

Preparing it — 6 steps (Griffin, 2015)

1 · Organize the planning process

Management commits and states the plan's objectives and uses; involve all key parties; assign responsibilities; set a timetable with milestones.

2 · Diagnose the situation

Evaluate the business: management team, markets and customers, products and marketing, production and operations, IT needs, financial standing and cash needs.

3 · Set goals

Use the diagnosis to set performance standards per strategy area; reconcile conflicting goals (e.g. marketing vs production, R&D vs finance) into one consistent framework.

4 · Develop operating plans

Translate strategies into specific plans for marketing, production/service, R&D, organization and management, IT — each with performance objectives.

5 · Develop a financial plan

From the operating objectives, build pro forma statements: income statements (quarterly/monthly), balance sheets (quarterly/annual), monthly cash-flow summaries, plus sales and budget schedules and indicators of profitability, growth, solvency and return on investment.

6 · Write the document

Not creative writing: present the analyses and decisions simply and concisely — the more verbiage, the more it reflects decisions not made. Start with an executive summary; include the market position and outlook.

The six major strategies every plan should address: market · production or service · research and development (R&D) · organization and management · financial · information technology (IT).

Contingency planning (for requirement 12)

What it is

"A plan designed to take account of a possible future event or circumstance" — for an emergency or something that might cause problems. Mitigation avoids risk; a contingency plan is what a firm does if the risk occurs. It can cover positive surprises too (better-than-expected demand).

How to build one

Identify scenarios with "what if" questions · use optimistic – normal – pessimistic/worst-case scenarios · identify the activities, tasks and major expense categories · add a buffer for expenses · set priorities · describe how responsibilities and resources would be reallocated · test the plan's assumptions.

📌 Linking it to the course

A bad local partner is a bounded reliability problem (H): opportunism or benevolent preference reversal. Contingency options come straight from G: switch partner, move to an EOR or a wholly owned entry (greenfield, M&A), or fall back to exporting; and from E: tighter payment terms (cash in advance, letter of credit) and a letter of guarantee.

Q1How does international finance differ from domestic finance?
Model

Above all, foreign-currency exposure. Also: different economic/political environment, different tax laws, stakeholders of different cultures/languages, the need for FX instruments (forwards/futures/options/swaps), GAAP reporting, and far more capital-raising options (a selection challenge). Domestic finance has none of these cross-border layers.

Q2What was Bretton Woods, and what changed in 1971?
Model

A 1944 system (40 nations) pegging currencies to the USD, itself convertible to gold ("as good as gold"), to stabilise post-WWII monetary exchange. In 1971 the US ended dollar-gold convertibility → floating currencies. It created the IMF and World Bank (the Bretton Woods twins).

☐ 1944/40 nations ☐ USD–gold peg ☐ 1971 end ☐ floating
Q3An exporter faces an unknown, risky foreign buyer. Which payment method, and why?
Model

Cash in advance is safest for the seller (paid before shipping); a letter of credit is the strong fallback (a bank pays against documents). Open account and consignment favour the buyer and suit trusted relationships. With an unknown risky buyer, insist on cash in advance or an L/C.

☐ cash in advance / L/C ☐ why (seller protection) ☐ contrast with open account
Q4Distinguish ADR from GDR, and name the three sources of international financing.

ADR = depository receipt issued & traded only in the USA (for US residents); GDR = issued across (mainly European)/multiple markets, denominated in USD. Both are negotiable receipts a bank issues against foreign shares. Three financing sources: commercial banks, international agencies & development banks (EXIM, EIB, EBRD, ADB), and the international capital market (GDR/ADR/IDR/FCCB).

🌙 Priority 1 — Finance & Culture (the deep pass)

Read these slowly. Each line is a named set.

🌙 International Finance

Int'l vs domestic (7): currency exposure first · different economic/political environment · different tax laws & regulations · stakeholders of different cultures/languages · forwards/futures/options/swaps needed · books per GAAP / accounting standards · many more capital options (so selecting is the challenge).

Bretton Woods: 1944, 40 nations, currencies pegged to the USD, USD convertible to gold → 1971 the US ends convertibility → floating rates. IMF + World Bank = the Bretton Woods twins.

6 exchange-rate determinants: inflation differential · interest-rate differentials · current-account deficits · public debt · terms of trade · political stability & economic performance. Plus the parities: PPP (law of one price) and interest rate parity (spot vs forward vs relative interest rates).

3 types of exchange risk: currency risk (day-to-day swings on fixed-price contracts) · business risk (long-run shifts in relative economic conditions) · translation risk (foreign income translated back to USD for accounting). Separately, the two main risks of international finance: foreign-exchange risk + political risk.

4 FX instruments: forward — agree today to exchange a set quantity on a set date at an agreed price; execution is mandatory · future — seller agrees to deliver a specific quantity/grade at a fixed future time, at a price agreed when the contract is entered · option — the buyer pays for the right, not the obligation, to buy or sell at an agreed price · swap — a spot purchase (sale) combined with a forward sale (purchase); types: interest-rate and currency swaps. ⧉ The deck defines these; using them to lock in the rate on a future foreign-currency payment (hedging) is the standard application, not spelled out on the slides.

5 payment methods: cash in advance · letter of credit · documentary collection · open account · consignment & trade finance. Seller-safest at the top, buyer-safest toward the bottom (⧉ the ordering is a memory aid, not slide content).

3 financing sources: commercial banks · agencies & development banks (EXIM, EIB, EBRD, ADB) · international capital market → GDR (USD, multi-market, no vote) · ADR (US only) · IDR (India, rupees) · FCCB (bond convertible to equity).

Institutions: IMF = stability & balance of payments, quota-weighted votes · World Bank = long-term development loans, 189 members. FDI = lasting interest + significant influence abroad; 4 types: horizontal · vertical · conglomerate · platform.

🌙 Culture & Negotiation

The 10 negotiation dimensions: objectives (contract ↔ relationship) · attitudes (win-lose ↔ win-win) · personal style (informal ↔ formal) · communication (direct ↔ indirect) · time sensitivity (high ↔ low) · emotionalism (high ↔ low) · agreement form (specific ↔ general) · agreement building (bottom-up ↔ top-down) · team organisation (one leader ↔ consensus) · risk taking (high ↔ low). A "contrast two cultures" answer runs down these rows.

The country notes are the slides' negotiation tendencies, not rules about individuals. Describe the difference and adapt to it; don't judge it.

🇨🇳 China — negotiating style: face (reputation, gained or lost) · respect for hierarchy (rank order in and out) · relationship & trust before the deal · long negotiations past deadlines · indirect "no" ("I'll need more time") · cards with both hands · gifts need checking (bribery risk with officials).

🇮🇳 India — negotiating style: family-owned business (up to 70%) · relationship before business, often over food/hospitality · flexible time · counterparts try to please and find "no" hard → be clear and direct.

🇩🇪 Germany — negotiating style: organised, agenda-driven, punctual · formal address (Herr/Frau + title), work/private kept apart · direct, fact-based, little small talk · risk-averse · binding decisions at the top, slowly · delivering on promises matters.

🇺🇸 USA — negotiating style: strict business culture (hard work expected) · law varies by state · humour/irony in meetings can come across as flippant · strong regional variation · little holiday time → keen to get things done.

The lesson: home-culture norms don't travel — Walmart left Germany after running US values there.

🌙 Priority 2 — Expansion & Theory (a lighter recall)

🌙 Expansion & Entry Modes

The 7 modes on one axis — control vs cost/risk: exporting & licensing (fast, low control) → partnerships/EOR → greenfield & M&A (full control, high commitment). Plus the 3 elements that must come together first, the risks (poor market understanding — Tesco/Starbucks; non-compliance), and trade missions.

🌙 Theory & the M&A model

Moskovicz's relevance-weighted country model (King 2004: no single variable predicts post-acquisition performance); internationalization = the firm side (national resource availability — the slide's "theory of competitive advantage" — and Porter's national diamond) vs the industry side (wider market, more competitors, lower entry barriers, more buyer power); bounded rationality (knowing) vs bounded reliability (doing); FSAs location-bound vs non-location-bound.

🌙 Midterm 1 — read once

One straight read-through of the four blocks below, no drilling. They're the foundation a case answer can lean on.

🌙 Strategy

Strategy is the long-term plan for a favourable position (tactics are the moves within it). The authors — Porter, Collis & Rustag, Grant — and Dess et al.: strategy = analyses, decisions, actions, aimed at a sustainable advantage. Positioning is the present; direction is the future.

🌙 Trade

Why countries trade: absolute advantage (Smith), comparative advantage (Ricardo, opportunity cost), factor endowments (Heckscher-Ohlin), varieties & scale (Krugman), and firms (Melitz). Plus Blonigen & Wilson's shift from US–Europe to Asia–US.

🌙 Free trade & protection

The bloc ladder: preferential → free trade area → customs union (+ common wall) → common market (+ resources move) → economic union (+ policy/currency) → full integration. Free trade's benefits and the protection toolkit: tariffs, quotas, product standards, subsidies — and dumping.

🌙 Locating production

Concentrate vs decentralize, decided by technological factors (fixed costs, minimum efficient scale, flexible manufacturing) and product factors (value-to-weight ratio, universal needs). The catch: real decisions (like autos) are rarely clear-cut.
