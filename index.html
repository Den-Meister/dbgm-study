Block A · 11 August

Strategy & International Business Foundations

What "international business" and "strategy" actually mean, how strategy differs from tactics, the author definitions, and the frameworks for describing and locating strategy.

Core definitions (memorize the shape, say them your way)

International — relating to or affecting two or more nations.
Business — an occupation, profession, or trade; the purchase and sale of goods/services in an attempt to make a profit; a profit-seeking enterprise.
International business — any business that operates across international borders. At its most basic, the sale of goods and services between countries — but other forms also exist.
Corporate strategy — a unique, long-term plan or framework designed to gain a competitive advantage over other market participants while delivering on customer/client and stakeholder promises. It guides organizational decisions (hiring, new products, entering new markets).
International business strategy — plans that guide commercial transactions between entities in different countries; the plans/policies of private companies (not governments) to achieve an overall goal — e.g. increased profits.

Strategy vs. Tactics

Strategy
The overall plan

The means by which individuals or organizations achieve their objectives; the overall plan for deploying resources to establish a favorable position. Long-term. (Military root: the science and art of command applied to the overall conduct of warfare.)

Tactic
The specific maneuver

A maneuver or action calculated to achieve some end; a scheme for a specific maneuver. Near-future.

📌 The 3 manager types (his analogy)

Creatives — "Let's go to Shanghai." Decision-makers — "OK, let's go tomorrow." Executives — which transport, the route, the timing. (Idea → decision → execution.)

Characteristics of strategic decisions

They are important, they involve a significant commitment of resources, and they are not easily reversible.

Strategy defined by authors (name = year = one idea)

Porter (2000)
Unique position

Strategy is the creation of a unique and valuable position, involving a different set of activities.

Collis & Rustag (2008)
State it simply

Most executives can't articulate the objective, scope, and advantage of their business in a simple statement — and if they can't, neither can anyone else.

Grant (2010)
Practice-driven

Business strategy's evolution has been driven more by the practical needs of business than by theory. In the 1950s–60s, execs struggled to coordinate decisions and keep control in ever larger, more complex firms.

Dess, McNamara, Eisner & Lee (2021)

📌 Where strategy actually lives (3 places)

1. In the heads of the CEO & senior managers (the ideal, rarely achieved). 2. In their articulations — speeches & documents (but firms never disclose confidential next steps). 3. In the decisions through which strategy is enacted (which outsiders can't fully read). Lesson: if you're not at the decision table, you see only a partial view, so your conclusions are only partially right.

📌 Two signature analogies

Company as a living thing (10 stages): 1 Newborn (foundation) · 2 Childhood (consolidation) · 3 Growth (development) · 4 Seeking a partner (alliances) · 5 Engagement (agreements/contracts) · 6 Marriage (M&A) · 7 Sons (split/spin-off) · 8 Maturity (profits fixed) · 9 Separation (divestiture/exit) · 10 Passing away (hostile M&A, bankruptcy).
Political parties ≈ companies: spread ideas = products/services; conquer voters = market share; leadership struggle = managers & directors; alliances with rivals = M&A; members' interest vs. party = the Agency Problem; splits = divestitures; ex-leaders found rivals = former-CEO entrepreneurship.

Block B · 18 August

International Trade

The models that explain why countries trade, the empirical patterns, and the actors and legal framework of international trade.

International trade — foreign trade: the exchange of goods, services, capital and intellectual/personal rights between individuals of different states. Two senses: (1) the turnover of goods between countries; (2) also the turnover of services, capital, labor and information.
Trade pattern — the composition of a country's imports and exports, and the volume of its trade with the rest of the world, which is likely to change over time.
Trade pattern theories — two theories explain patterns of trade: comparative advantage and increasing returns to scale.

Framework — the models of international trade (the spine of this block)

ModelAuthor(s)Core idea
Absolute advantageAdam Smith (1776, Wealth of Nations)Reply to mercantilism (grow wealth by pushing exports, blocking imports). Unfettered trade benefits both importer and exporter — each produces what it makes best.
Comparative advantageRicardoTwo-country, two-good example: countries gain from trade even if one has an absolute advantage in both. Specialize in the good with the lowest opportunity cost and export it. Predicts specialization & direction of trade.
Heckscher-OhlinHeckscher & OhlinBest-known alternative to Ricardo. Comparative advantage comes from differing factor endowments and how intensively sectors use them. H-O theorem: a country exports the good that uses intensively the factor it has in relative abundance.
New trade theoryKrugman (1979)Most world trade is between similar developed countries and much of it is intra-industry (two-way, same industry). Monopolistic firms each make a different variety + economies of scale; consumers have a "love for variety." Countries trade varieties even with no comparative advantage.
Recent advancesMelitz (2003)Micro-foundations: firms are heterogeneous in productive efficiency; models which firms decide to export, affecting industry dynamics, aggregate productivity, and wages.
⚠️ Which model for which trade?

New trade theory best explains trade between similar developed countries and intra-industry trade (the majority of world trade). Comparative-advantage models best explain trade between developed and less-developed countries. The gravity equation can be derived from both.

Empirical patterns — Blonigen & Wilson (2012), 4 findings

1
Phenomenal growth

Trade is growing at a phenomenal rate.

2
Few dominant players

Trade is dominated by relatively few countries, who tend to stay dominant.

3
Uneven but stable

Growth rates differ a lot across products, yet the relative sizes of product markets stay stable.

4
Shift to Asia

Over 40 years, major flows shifted from US–Europe to Asia–US.

International trade patterns — the 8 terms he lists

Trade FlowInternational EconomyComparative AdvantageForeign InvestmentForeign SubsidiaryInstitutional ChangeRisk PremiumForeign Economic Policy

📌 Globalization to the consumer

More choices, generally lower prices (not always), and a blurred national identity of products: a Volvo owned by China's Geely, a Jaguar owned by India's Tata, a Dodge from Italy's Fiat, foreign brands built in the US. The point: brands and production are decoupled across borders.

Actors of international trade (the legal-framework slide)

States

Key, indisputable creators of international law. Most are both de jure (in law) and de facto (in reality) states. Developed vs. developing countries take different approaches.

Corporations

Often multinationals; profit-driven; lobby states & international organizations to protect their interests — with the same accountability/legitimacy questions raised about NGOs.

International organizations

Intergovernmental orgs / NGOs formed between governments; decide by one-state-one-vote, consensus, or weighted voting.

Regional organizations

Regional bodies coordinating trade among neighboring states.

Juridical & physical persons

Individuals increasingly hold rights and responsibilities under international law (e.g. human-rights law).

Arbitration associations

Handle dispute settlement — often preferred because foreign arbitration awards enjoy a single, near-universal enforcement regime.

📌 International commercial contracts

A contract is "international" when two or more states conclude an agreement (e.g. the Vienna 1980 UN Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods; the Hague Principles 2015). Parties may choose the governing law; if they don't, a court applies conflict-of-laws rules. Arbitration is common because foreign arbitral decisions have an almost inclusive enforcement regime.

Produce it — practice

CompareExplain absolute vs. comparative advantage, with the authors.
Model answer

Absolute advantage (Smith, 1776): a country should produce and export what it makes most efficiently; trade benefits both sides — a reply to mercantilism. Comparative advantage (Ricardo): even if one country is absolutely better at both goods, both still gain by specializing in the good with the lower opportunity cost and trading for the other. Key difference: comparative advantage shows gains exist even without an absolute edge, and it predicts specialization and the direction of trade.

☐ Smith 1776 ☐ Ricardo ☐ "opportunity cost" ☐ "even if one has absolute advantage in both" ☐ difference stated
RecallState the Heckscher-Ohlin theorem in one sentence.
Model answer

A country will export the good that uses relatively intensively the factor of production in which that country is relatively abundant — comparative advantage coming from differing factor endowments.

☐ "abundant factor" ☐ "used intensively" ☐ ties to factor endowments
ApplyWhy do two similar developed countries (say Germany & France) trade cars with each other? Which model explains it?
Model answer

Krugman's new trade theory (1979). They have similar factor endowments, so classic comparative advantage doesn't predict much trade. But firms produce different varieties of cars under economies of scale, and consumers have a "love for variety." So the two countries engage in intra-industry (two-way) trade — each importing the other's varieties — gaining from more variety and larger-scale production.

☐ Krugman/new trade theory ☐ intra-industry / varieties ☐ economies of scale ☐ love for variety

Model answer

1. Trade is growing at a phenomenal rate. 2. Trade is dominated by relatively few countries, who stay dominant. 3. Product growth rates differ widely, yet relative market sizes stay stable. 4. Over 40 years, flows shifted from US–Europe to Asia–US.

Block C · 25 August

Free Trade, Trade Blocs & Protectionism

The levels of economic integration, why free trade helps (and hurts), the major blocs, and the toolkit of protectionism.

Framework — levels of integration (know the escalating differences)

Free Trade AreaCustoms UnionSingle Market
No tariffs between members. No common external tariff (so border checks on country of origin). Each member can negotiate its own trade deals. No tariffs between members. Common external tariff. Trade deals negotiated for the whole union. No tariffs, no border checks. Freedom of movement of goods and people. Common rules and regulations.
⚠️ The distinguishing features

What separates them: external tariff (FTA = none/own deals; Customs Union = common) and movement + common rules (only the Single Market adds free movement of people and shared regulation). If asked to compare, structure by those two axes.

Free trade — policies permitting inexpensive imports and exports without tariffs or other trade barriers. A free trade agreement (FTA) is a group of countries agreeing to lower tariffs/barriers to trade more with each other.

What FTAs actually do

Reduce/eliminate tariffs · address behind-the-border barriers · encourage investment · improve rules (e.g. intellectual property) · enhance e-commerce and government procurement.

Benefits of free trade — Pettinger (2019) (8 points)

1
Comparative advantage

Specialize where opportunity cost is lower → higher welfare for all.

2
Trade creation

Lower tariffs shift consumption from high-cost to low-cost producers.

3
Increased exports

Lower foreign tariffs → more exports → jobs and growth.

4
Economies of scale

Specializing → larger scale → lower average costs → lower prices.

5
Increased competition

More rivals → cut costs, more efficiency, curbs domestic monopolies.

6
Engine of growth

World trade has grown ~7% a year since 1945.

7
Use of surplus raw materials

Qatar's oil is only valuable with trade; resource-poor Japan needs trade to avoid low GDP.

8
Tariffs breed inefficiency

Protected industries have little incentive to cut costs.

⚠️ Free trade — the downsides he lists

Harmful to the environment · bad working conditions · intellectual-property theft · job outsourcing · degradation of natural resources. (FTA pros/cons framing: PROS — growth, lower government spending, technology transfer; CONS — outsourcing, poor working conditions, resource degradation.)

Major regional trade agreements (by share of global GDP, per his chart)

RCEP ≈ 30.5%USMCA ≈ 28%EU ≈ 17.9%AfCFTA ≈ 2.9%Mercosur ≈ 2.4%

📌 RCEP — the headline bloc

The Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership: a free trade agreement among 15 Asia-Pacific nations, officially the biggest trading bloc in history — ~30% of global GDP and population, larger than USMCA and the EU. India pulled out. It sets rules on investment, competition, telecommunications, intellectual property, and e-commerce.

Framework — the protectionist toolkit

Tool 1
Tariffs

Charged to the importing country; raise the price of imports. Three types: Scientific (item-by-item), Peril-point (industry-specific — the level beyond which a tariff change seriously harms an industry), Retaliatory (response to excessive duties by partners).

Tool 2
Import quotas

Non-tariff barrier limiting the quantity imported over a period. Can prevent dumping. An embargo (total ban) is the most severe quota.

Tool 3
Product standards

Safety/quality rules that block imports failing internal controls. Example: French raw-milk cheeses aged under 60 days are banned in the US — protecting US producers.

Tool 4
Government subsidies

Direct (cash payments) or indirect (interest-free loans, tax breaks). Export subsidies push domestic firms to expand abroad.

Dumping — when foreign firms sell products at artificially low prices (below production cost) in a market, often due to unfair subsidies or overproduction.

Protectionism — the balance sheet

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Protect industry & jobs · protect the consumer · retaliation against unfair competition · national security · better balance of trade · shields emerging domestic industries. Lack of economic efficiency · less consumer choice · retaliation risk · higher prices → inflation, lower real incomes · lower productivity & innovation. Broad economist consensus: protectionism harms growth/welfare; free trade helps.
📌 Real-world examples

EU Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) tariffs protecting farmers · US tariffs on Chinese tyres · the "Banana Wars." Protectionist-measure counts: China 200–300, the US over 800, UK & Germany ~300 each; most measures target China (a "new trade war"). Per Credit Suisse / Global Trade Alert, the most protectionist country is the US.

Produce it — practice

CompareDistinguish a free trade area, a customs union, and a single market.
Model answer

Free trade area: no tariffs between members, no common external tariff (so origin checks), each member negotiates its own external deals. Customs union: no internal tariffs plus a common external tariff, with trade deals negotiated for the whole union. Single market: all of the above plus no border checks, free movement of goods and people, and common rules and regulations. Axis of escalation: external tariff, then free movement + shared regulation.

ApplyA country bans a cheaper foreign cheese on "safety" grounds. What tool is this, and what's the real effect?
Model answer

A product standard — a non-tariff protectionist barrier. Framed as safety/quality (his example: French raw-milk cheeses aged under 60 days banned in the US), its real effect is to block imports and shift volume to domestic producers, protecting them from cheaper competition.

☐ names "product standard" ☐ non-tariff barrier ☐ real effect = protect domestic producers
IntegrationArgue both sides: is protectionism justified? Structure it.
Model answer

For: protects industry & jobs, shields emerging domestic industries, national security, better balance of trade, defends against unfair competition/dumping. Against: economic inefficiency, less consumer choice, higher prices → inflation and lower real incomes, weaker productivity and innovation, and retaliation risk. Consensus: economists broadly find protectionism harms growth and welfare while free trade helps — so it's mainly justified as a targeted, temporary defense (infant industry, security), not a general policy.
