Block A

Strategy & International Business Foundations

What "international business" and "strategy" mean, how strategy differs from tactics, the author definitions, and the frameworks for describing and locating strategy.

Core definitions (memorize the shape, say them your way)

International — relating to or affecting two or more nations.
Business — an occupation, profession, or trade; the purchase and sale of goods/services to make a profit; a profit-seeking enterprise.
International business — any business that operates across international borders. At its most basic, the sale of goods and services between countries — but other forms also exist.
Corporate strategy — a unique, long-term plan/framework designed to gain competitive advantage over other market participants while delivering on customer/client and stakeholder promises. Guides decisions like hiring, new products, entering markets.
International business strategy — plans guiding commercial transactions between entities in different countries; the plans/policies of private companies (not governments) to achieve an overall goal — e.g. increased profits.

Strategy vs. Tactics

Strategy
The overall plan

The means by which people or organizations achieve their objectives; the overall plan for deploying resources to establish a favorable position. Long-term. (Military root: the science and art of command applied to the overall conduct of warfare.)

Tactic
The specific maneuver

A maneuver or action calculated to achieve some end; a scheme for a specific maneuver. Near-future.

📌 The 3 manager types (his analogy)

Creatives — "Let's go to Shanghai." Decision-makers — "OK, let's go tomorrow." Executives — which transport, the route, the timing. (Idea → decision → execution.)

Characteristics of strategic decisions

They are important, involve a significant commitment of resources, and are not easily reversible.

Strategy defined by authors — each name paired with its one idea

Porter (2000)
Unique position

Strategy is the creation of a unique and valuable position, involving a different set of activities.

Cite for: strategy = choosing a different set of activities, not just doing the same ones better.

Collis & Rustag (2008)
State it simply

Most executives can't articulate the objective, scope, and advantage of their business in a simple statement — and if they can't, neither can anyone else.

Cite for: the test of a real strategy is whether it fits one clear sentence.

Grant (2010)
Practice-driven

Business strategy's evolution is driven more by the practical needs of business than by theory. In the 1950s–60s, execs struggled to coordinate decisions and keep control in ever larger, more complex firms.

Cite for: strategy theory follows business practice — it grew from managers' real coordination problems.

Dess, McNamara, Eiser & Lee (2021)

📌 Where strategy actually lives (3 places)

1. In the heads of the CEO & senior managers (ideal, rarely achieved). 2. In their articulations — speeches & documents (but firms never disclose confidential next steps). 3. Block B

International Trade

The models that explain why countries trade, the empirical patterns, and the actors and legal framework of international trade.

International trade — foreign trade: the exchange of goods, services, capital and intellectual/personal rights between individuals of different states. Two senses: (1) turnover of goods between countries; (2) also turnover of services, capital, labor and information.
Trade pattern — the composition of a country's imports and exports, and the volume of its trade with the rest of the world, which is likely to change over time.
Trade pattern theories — two theories explain patterns of trade: comparative advantage and increasing returns to scale.

Framework — the models of international trade (the spine of this block)

ModelAuthor(s)Core idea
Absolute advantageAdam Smith (1776)
Wealth of Nations		Reply to mercantilism (grow wealth by pushing exports, blocking imports). Unfettered trade benefits both importer and exporter — each produces what it makes best.
Comparative advantageRicardoTwo-country, two-good example: countries gain even if one has an absolute advantage in both. Specialize in the good with the lowest opportunity cost and export it. Predicts specialization & direction of trade.
Heckscher-OhlinHeckscher & OhlinBest-known alternative to Ricardo. Advantage comes from differing factor endowments and how intensively sectors use them. H-O theorem: a country exports the good using intensively the factor it has in relative abundance.
New trade theoryKrugman (1979)Most world trade is between similar developed countries and much is intra-industry (two-way, same industry). Monopolistic firms each make a variety + economies of scale; consumers have a "love for variety." Countries trade varieties even with no comparative advantage.
Recent advancesMelitz (2003)Micro-foundations: firms are heterogeneous in productive efficiency; models which firms export, affecting industry dynamics, aggregate productivity, and wages.

Use in an answer: name the author + the one distinguishing idea; if asked which model fits a given case, apply the rule in the box below.

⚠️ Which model for which trade?

New trade theory best explains trade between similar developed countries and intra-industry trade (most of world trade). Comparative-advantage models best explain developed ↔ less-developed trade. The gravity equation can be derived from both.

Empirical patterns — Blonigen & Wilson (2012), 4 findings

1 · Phenomenal growth

Trade is growing at a phenomenal rate.

2 · Few dominant players

Trade is dominated by relatively few countries, who tend to stay dominant.

3 · Uneven but stable

Growth rates differ a lot across products, yet relative product-market sizes stay stable.

4 · Shift to Asia

Over 40 years, major flows shifted from US–Europe to Asia–US.

📌 Globalization to the consumer

More choices, generally lower prices (not always), and a blurred national identity of products: a Volvo owned by China's Geely, a Jaguar owned by India's Tata, a Dodge from Italy's Fiat, foreign brands built in the US. The point: brands and production are decoupled across borders.

Actors of international trade (the legal-framework slide)

States

Key, indisputable creators of international law. Most are both de jure (in law) and de facto (in reality). Developed vs. developing take different approaches.

Corporations

Often multinationals; profit-driven; lobby states & international organizations to protect their interests — with the same accountability questions raised about NGOs.

International organizations

Intergovernmental orgs / NGOs formed between governments; decide by one-state-one-vote, consensus, or weighted voting.

Regional organizations

Regional bodies coordinating trade among neighboring states.

Juridical & physical persons

Individuals increasingly hold rights and responsibilities under international law (e.g. human-rights law).

Arbitration associations

Handle dispute settlement — often preferred because foreign arbitral awards enjoy a single, near-universal enforcement regime.

📌 International commercial contracts

A contract is "international" when two or more states conclude an agreement (e.g. the Vienna 1980 UN Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods; the Hague Principles 2015). Parties may choose the governing law; if they don't, a court applies conflict-of-laws rules. Arbitration is common because foreign arbitral decisions have an almost inclusive enforcement regime.

CompareExplain absolute vs. comparative advantage, with the authors.
Model answer

Absolute advantage (Smith, 1776): a country should produce and export what it makes most efficiently; trade benefits both sides — a reply to mercantilism. Comparative advantage (Ricardo): even if one country is absolutely better at both goods, both still gain by specializing in the good with the lower opportunity cost and trading. Key difference: comparative advantage shows gains exist even without an absolute edge, and predicts specialization and direction of trade.

ExplainHow does the Heckscher-Ohlin model explain trade?
Model answer

Comparative advantage arises from differing factor endowments. The H-O theorem: a country exports the good that uses intensively the factor of production in which it is relatively abundant (and imports the other). It differs from Ricardo by locating advantage in what a country has, not in productivity differences alone.

☐ factor endowments ☐ abundant factor used intensively ☐ contrast to Ricardo
ApplyWhy do two similar developed countries trade cars with each other? Which model explains it?
Model answer

Krugman's new trade theory (1979). Similar factor endowments mean classic comparative advantage predicts little trade. But firms produce different varieties under economies of scale, and consumers have a "love for variety." So they engage in intra-industry (two-way) trade — each importing the other's varieties — gaining from more variety and larger-scale production.

☐ Krugman/new trade theory ☐ intra-industry/varieties ☐ economies of scale ☐ love for variety

Block C

Free Trade, Trade Blocs & Protectionism

The levels of economic integration, why free trade helps (and hurts), the major blocs, and the toolkit of protectionism.

The integration ladder — the full spectrum of trade blocs

A trade bloc is a group of countries that agree to reduce trade barriers among themselves. They differ along two axes: internal rules (how far barriers between members fall) and external rules (how members treat non-members). Read it as a ladder — each rung keeps everything below it and adds one new thing. Name what each rung adds and you can answer any "distinguish them" question.

1 · Preferential Trade Area
Lower, don't eliminate

Members lower but do not eliminate barriers among themselves. Non-members aren't addressed — each keeps its own policy toward them. The lowest level of commitment.

2 · Free Trade Area
Eliminate internal barriers

Members eliminate barriers among themselves but keep independent external policies. Ex: NAFTA (Canada, Mexico, US).

3 · Customs Union
+ common external barriers

Eliminate internal barriers and adopt common external barriers toward non-members. Ex: Russia, Kazakhstan & Belarus (customs union, 2010).

4 · Common Market
+ resources move freely

Everything above plus free movement of resources (e.g. labor) among members. Ex: Mercosur (Southern Cone Market); East/West African Common Markets.

5 · Economic Union
+ one economic policy

Everything above plus a uniform set of economic policies; a common currency forces a common monetary policy. Ex: the European Union once the euro was adopted.

6 · Full integration
One state

Complete integration of the member states into a single unified economy. Ex: the United States.

⚠️ How to answer "distinguish them"

Walk up the ladder and name what each rung adds: lower barriers (PTA) → eliminate them (FTA) → common external wall (Customs Union) → free movement of resources (Common Market) → uniform economic policy/currency (Economic Union) → one state (full integration). The single most-tested jump is the common external tariff (FTA → Customs Union).

🌍 Two naming systems in your materials — don't blur them

Your sources label these stages two different ways, and either can appear on the exam. Keep them straight:

25 Aug slide — 3 tiers: Free Trade Area → Customs Union → Single Market. Its Single Market = no tariffs, no border checks, free movement of goods AND people, plus common rules & regulations.

The video — 6 tiers: Preferential → Free Trade Area → Customs Union → Common Market → Economic Union → Full integration. Its Common Market adds free movement of resources (e.g. labor); Economic Union adds a uniform policy/currency.

Crosswalk: the slide's "Single Market" sits high on the video's ladder — it covers the Common Market (free movement) plus the common-rules part of Economic Union. Rule of thumb: answer in whatever term the question uses and give that term's exact definition — don't merge "Common Market" and "Single Market" into one.

Free trade — policies permitting inexpensive imports and exports without tariffs or other trade barriers. A free trade agreement (FTA) is a group of countries agreeing to lower tariffs/barriers to trade more with each other.

An FTA reaches further than "no tariffs"

The point to make in an answer: a modern FTA doesn't stop at the tariff line — it reaches behind the border. It reduces/eliminates tariffs, tackles behind-the-border barriers, encourages investment, tightens rules like intellectual property, and opens up e-commerce and government procurement. So "free trade agreement" means deeper economic rule-setting, not just cheaper imports.

Benefits of free trade — Pettinger (2019)

1 · Comparative advantage

Specialize where opportunity cost is lower → higher welfare for all.

2 · Trade creation

Lower tariffs shift consumption from high-cost to low-cost producers.

3 · Increased exports

Lower foreign tariffs → more exports → jobs and growth.

4 · Economies of scale

Specializing → larger scale → lower average costs → lower prices.

5 · Increased competition

More rivals → cut costs, more efficiency, curbs domestic monopolies.

6 · Engine of growth

World trade has grown ~7% a year since 1945.

7 · Use of surplus raw materials

Qatar's oil is only valuable with trade; resource-poor Japan needs trade to avoid low GDP.

8 · Tariffs breed inefficiency

Protected industries have little incentive to cut costs.

⚠️ Free trade — the downsides he lists

Harmful to the environment · bad working conditions · intellectual-property theft · job outsourcing · degradation of natural resources. (PROS/CONS framing: PROS — growth, lower government spending, technology transfer; CONS — outsourcing, poor working conditions, resource degradation.)

Major regional trade agreements (share of global GDP, per his chart)

RCEP ≈ 30.5%USMCA ≈ 28%EU ≈ 17.9%AfCFTA ≈ 2.9%Mercosur ≈ 2.4%

Memory hook: the top three are neck-and-neck and dwarf the rest — RCEP (Asia) just ahead of USMCA (North America), then the EU; AfCFTA and Mercosur are a distant tier (~2–3% each). One line to remember: the world's biggest trade bloc by GDP is Asia's, not the West's.

📌 RCEP — the headline bloc

The Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership: a free trade agreement among 15 Asia-Pacific nations, officially the biggest trading bloc in history — ~30% of global GDP and population, larger than USMCA and the EU. India pulled out. It sets rules on investment, competition, telecommunications, intellectual property, and e-commerce.

Framework — the protectionist toolkit

Tool 1 · Tariffs

Charged to the importing country; raise the price of imports. Three types: Scientific (item-by-item), Peril-point (industry-specific harm threshold), Retaliatory (response to partners' excessive duties).

Tool 2 · Import quotas

Non-tariff barrier limiting the quantity imported over a period. Can prevent dumping. An embargo (total ban) is the most severe quota.

Tool 3 · Product standards

Safety/quality rules that block imports failing internal controls. Example: French raw-milk cheeses aged under 60 days are banned in the US — protecting US producers.

Tool 4 · Government subsidies

Direct (cash payments) or indirect (interest-free loans, tax breaks). Export subsidies push domestic firms to expand abroad.

Dumping — when foreign firms sell products at artificially low prices (below production cost) in a market, often due to unfair subsidies or overproduction.

Protectionism — the balance sheet

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Protect industry & jobs · protect the consumer · retaliation against unfair competition · national security · better balance of trade · shields emerging domestic industries. Lack of economic efficiency · less consumer choice · retaliation risk · higher prices → inflation, lower real incomes · lower productivity & innovation. Broad economist consensus: protectionism harms growth/welfare; free trade helps.
📌 Real-world examples

EU Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) tariffs protecting farmers · US tariffs on Chinese tyres · the Banana Wars. Protectionist-measure counts: China 200–300, the US over 800, UK & Germany ~300 each; most measures target China (a "new trade war"). Per Credit Suisse / Global Trade Alert, the most protectionist country is the US.

Block D · course video

Strategies for Locating Production Facilities

Where should an international firm put its production to minimize cost and improve quality? Beyond country-level factors, weigh technological factors and product factors — then choose to concentrate or decentralize.

Source: the course video "Strategies for Locating Production Facilities," shown in class.

Technological factors — 3 traits of a manufacturing technology

1 · Fixed costs
How costly to set up a plant

High fixed costs → serve the world from one / few locations (e.g. a $5B semiconductor plant). Low → produce in many locations: local responsiveness + a hedge against adverse currency moves.

2 · Minimum efficient scale
Where scale economies run out

The output level at which most plant-level scale economies are exhausted. Large MES vs global demand → centralize. Low MES → several plants (smartphones: MES ≈ 5M/yr vs demand >1B/yr → Apple, Samsung, Huawei assemble in many sites).

3 · Flexible manufacturing system
Adapts to type & quantity

Machines/computers reconfigure to make varied products and enable make-to-order customization; raises efficiency and lowers cost — but higher upfront equipment cost.

Product factors — 2 features of the product itself

1 · Value-to-weight ratio
Monetary value per kg

High (electronics, pharmaceuticals) → transport is a tiny % of cost → concentrate in the optimal location and serve the world. Low (refined sugar, bulk chemicals, paint, petroleum) → transport is a big % → produce near major markets.

2 · Universal needs
Same tastes worldwide?

International Finance

How money moves between countries — exchange rates, financing sources, instruments, institutions and FDI — and how all of that differs from doing finance at home.

International finance (a.k.a. international macroeconomics) — the study of monetary interactions between two or more countries, focusing on foreign direct investment and currency exchange rates. It uses mostly macroeconomic methods (international trade theory is more microeconomic). Key theories: the Mundell-Fleming model, optimum currency area (OCA) theory, and purchasing power parity (PPP).

Bretton Woods — the system behind it all

📌 Bretton Woods (1944)

Emerged from a 1944 conference of 40 nations to standardize international monetary exchange and nurture post-WWII stability. Members settled cross-border bills in US-dollar-denominated bills exchangeable for gold ("as good as gold"); every member currency was pegged to the USD. The US ended dollar-gold convertibility in 1971 → currencies became floating. The IMF and World Bank are the "Bretton Woods twins."

What moves exchange rates — 6 determinants

1 · Inflation differential

Relative inflation between the two countries.

2 · Interest-rate differentials

Relative interest rates.

3 · Current account deficits

Balance of payments position.

4 · Public debt

Government indebtedness.

5 · Terms of trade

Export prices vs import prices.

6 · Political stability & economic performance

Confidence in the economy.

📌 Parity relationships

Purchasing Power Parity (PPP) — the price of an item is the same regardless of the currency used to buy it (the law of one price); it explains what drives exchange-rate changes over time. Interest Rate Parity — the relationship between spot rates, forward rates and relative interest rates. A spot trade exchanges currency immediately; a forward contract agrees to exchange at a future date and set price.

FX risk — the four risks to name

Currency / transaction risk

Day-to-day exchange-rate swings on contracts to buy/sell at fixed prices in the short run.

Business risk

Long-run moves from unanticipated changes in relative economic conditions.

Translation risk

Foreign income must be translated back to USD for accounting even if not actually converted.

Political risk

Turmoil in one country hits stakeholders across the business.

In an answer: the two headline risks of international finance are foreign-exchange risk and political risk.

The 5 international payment methods

Cash in AdvanceLetter of CreditDocumentary CollectionOpen Account TermsConsignment & Trade Finance

Memory hook — buyer-risk ladder: Cash in Advance (safest for the seller) → Letter of Credit (bank guarantees) → Documentary Collection → Open Account (safest for the buyer) → Consignment (seller paid only after goods sell). Trust rises as you go down. ⧉ Outside — the slide only lists the five; this seller↔buyer risk ordering is a plain-language memory aid, not slide content.

Sources of international financing — 3 categories

A · Commercial banks

Provide foreign-currency loans & advances worldwide; the backbone of trade/export-import finance.

B · Agencies & development banks

Government-created to fund development on medium/long terms. Ex: EXIM Bank, European Investment Bank (EIB), EBRD, Asian Development Bank.

C · International capital market

The most-used source; raises funds via instruments like GDRs, ADRs, IDRs, FCCBs (below).

Capital-market instruments (the depository receipts & bonds)

GDR — Global Depository Receipt

Shares issued in the home currency, lodged with a depository bank that issues receipts (denominated in USD) traded on foreign exchanges. Attracts global investors; holder gets dividends but no voting rights; convertible to shares after 45 days.

ADR — American Depository Receipt

Like a GDR but issued & traded only in the USA, for US residents, listed only on US exchanges. Lets US investors hold overseas companies. Types: sponsored / unsponsored.

IDR — Indian Depository Receipt

The Indian version, denominated in rupees, available in Indian markets (regulator: SEBI).

FCCB — Foreign Currency Convertible Bond

Debt + equity hybrid: a bond (bought/sold in foreign markets, lower fixed interest) convertible into equity/depository receipts later; ~5-year redemption; dilutes ownership.

⚠️ ADR vs GDR

ADR = United States only. GDR = issued across (mainly European) markets / multiple countries. Both are negotiable certificates a depository bank issues against foreign shares; both help acquire resources & invest across borders.

International financial instruments — the families

Beyond receipts: International bonds (foreign bonds & euro bonds, global & straight bonds, convertible & cocktail bonds, floating-rate notes); short/medium-term (euro notes, mid-term euro notes, euro commercial paper); and the general toolkit — deposits, SDRs (Special Drawing Rights, IMF reserve assets), borrowings, loans, shares/equity, derivatives (options, swaps), letters of guarantee & credit, debentures/bonds.

The Bretton Woods institutions

IMF — International Monetary Fund

Promotes global economic growth & financial stability, coordinates currency exchange and the balance of payments, reduces poverty. Voting is quota-weighted by economic size (not one-country-one-vote).

World Bank

A cooperative of 189 member countries; provides development loans to developing countries to reduce poverty. Focus: long-term development (IMF = macro/financial stability).

World Bank Group (5 arms): IBRD · IDA · IFC · MIGA · ICSID. Together with the IMF = the Bretton Woods twins.

Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)

FDI — cross-border investment where an investor in one economy establishes a lasting interest in, and a significant degree of influence over, an enterprise in another economy.
4 types

Horizontal (same industry abroad — most common), Vertical (a supplier/buyer stage abroad), Conglomerate (unrelated business abroad), Platform (base to export to a third market).

Why it matters

Primary source of external capital & revenue; transfers technology/skills, creates jobs, drives growth — things trade or portfolio investment alone can't.

Global Expansion & Entry Modes

Deciding to grow beyond home borders — why firms do it, what to weigh, and the seven ways in, each with its own trade-off between speed, control, cost and risk.

Overseas / global expansion — a company's strategic decision to extend operations into foreign markets by establishing a physical or operational presence (sales offices, factories, service centres) to reach new customers, tap global talent and gain a competitive edge.

Why firms expand — and when they should

Why expand

Increased revenue (new segments) · cost reduction (cheaper production/labour) · risk mitigation (diversify beyond one market) · competitive pressure (keep pace) · access to resources/talent.

The 3 elements that must align

1. Traction — a signal of market potential. 2. Feasible profitability / interest. 3. Ability to invest in the right level of localization. "A signal to expand doesn't mean you should — weigh interest and capacity."

📌 Considerations before entering

Internationalization Theory & the M&A Evaluation Model

The deeper "why it's hard" layer: the professor's own country-evaluation model for M&A, how going international reshapes strategy, and the two limits every cross-border manager hits.

Prof. Moskovicz's M&A country-evaluation model

📌 "A Worldwide Business Evaluation Model for M&A" (Moskovicz, 2019)

His own paper. The idea: no single variable predicts whether an M&A (or market entry) will succeed, so you list all relevant variables, rate each, and weight them by relevance. For a country, score each variable Low / Med / High and Positive / Neutral / Negative, times a Relevance weight, and total it (the "National General Evaluation", done separately by sector).

Variables include: clear/stable commercial rules · technological development · communications · infrastructure · justice-court independence · political stability · macroeconomic stability · corruption level · protective laws / import barriers · unemployment · local-currency stability · rigid labour rules · investment trend · incentives for foreign investment · free access to FX · dividend taxation/limits for foreign firms.

⚠️ Why a weighted model (the authors)

Haleblian (2009): the causes of M&A failure are under-researched and need more study. King (2004): none of the strategic or financial variables alone predicts variance in post-acquisition performance. Conclusion: you need a holistic, relevance-weighted assessment — not one magic metric.

How internationalization reshapes strategy

The Firm — resources & capabilitiesThe Industry — environment
National resource availability (the theory of comparative advantage); the impact of national context on how resources develop — Porter's national diamond. A wider market with more diverse customer preferences; more competitors; lower entry barriers; increased buyer power.

Point: internationalization sits between firm and industry — it widens opportunity and intensifies competition.

The two limits every cross-border manager hits

Bounded rationality (Simon, 1982)

"Scarcity of mind": you never have complete information (especially about the future) and you can't fully process what you do have. For an IB strategist it bites in entry-mode choice and in the home-vs-host perspective gap — head office and subsidiary managers deem different information relevant (Verbeke & Yuan, 2005).

Bounded reliability (Verbeke, 2009)

Not about knowing but about doing: actors fail to fully deliver on promises. Sources (Kano & Verbeke, 2015): opportunism (self-interest, false promises) and benevolent preference reversal (good-faith promises abandoned as preferences change — e.g. subsidiary managers chasing local opportunities). Firms add safeguards/enforcement to detect and punish it.

📌 FSAs — firm-specific advantages (Verbeke, 2009)

A firm builds on the broad location advantages (LAs) of its home country → a smaller subset of location-bound (LB) FSAs → a still-smaller subset of non-location-bound (NLB) FSAs that can transfer across borders. Bounded rationality & reliability limit how well those NLB advantages transfer to the host country. Related: SSAs (subsidiary-specific) and ASAs (alliance-specific) advantages.

List every relevant variable (commercial rules, political/macro stability, corruption, infrastructure, FX access, incentives, labour rules…), rate each Low/Med/High and Positive/Neutral/Negative, and weight by relevance for an overall score (per sector). Rationale: King (2004) — no single strategic/financial variable predicts post-acquisition performance; Haleblian (2009) — failure causes are under-studied. So a holistic weighted assessment beats one metric.

☐ list+rate+weight ☐ per sector ☐ King no-single-predictor
Q7Distinguish bounded rationality from bounded reliability.
Model

Bounded rationality (Simon 1982) = limits on knowing/deciding: incomplete information + limited processing — bites in entry-mode choice and the home-vs-host perspective gap. Bounded reliability (Verbeke 2009) = limits on doing: promises aren't fully kept — via opportunism or benevolent preference reversal. Firms answer rationality with information and reliability with safeguards.

☐ rationality = know ☐ reliability = do ☐ an example/source each
Q8What are FSAs, and what does location-bound vs non-location-bound mean?
Model

FSAs = firm-specific advantages, built on the home country's broad location advantages. A subset are location-bound (LB) — tied to home; a smaller subset are non-location-bound (NLB) — transferable across borders. Bounded rationality & reliability limit how well NLB advantages actually transfer to a host country (Verbeke 2009).

☐ FSA defined ☐ LB vs NLB ☐ transfer limited by bounded rationality/reliability

Q9 · CaseA mid-size firm wants fast, low-risk, low-investment entry but fears losing brand control. Advise on entry mode.
Model

Decision: entry mode. Lens (G): exporting and licensing give fast, low-risk, low-cost entry but low control (licensing also risks brand damage). The middle path is a partnership — shared risk, local knowledge, still a local entity, more control than licensing. Full control (greenfield/M&A) contradicts "low investment." Recommend: a partnership if brand control matters most; otherwise export first, license later. Trade-off: control vs cost/risk.

Q10 · CaseYour team will negotiate the same deal in China and in Germany. Prepare by contrasting the two on the negotiation dimensions.
Model

Lens (F): Goal — China relationship-first vs Germany contract/task-focused. Communication — China indirect (protect face) vs Germany direct/logical. Recommend: in China build the personal bond and be patient; in Germany bring a precise, well-prepared, fact-based agenda. Don't impose home norms (Walmart's German failure).

Q11 · CaseA US firm sends a blunt team to close a deal in India in a single meeting. What goes wrong, and what should they do?
Model

Lens (F): India is family-oriented and relationship-first — deals need trust built over several meetings (meals, small talk), not a one-visit hard close. The US speed/directness skips trust-building; Indian counterparts (who find it hard to say "no") seem agreeable but won't commit. Fix: invest time in the relationship, accept flexible timing, read indirect cues, and build a personal bond before pushing the contract.

Q13 · CaseWhy do strong domestic firms (e.g. Walmart, Tesco, Starbucks) fail when they expand abroad?
Model

In theory terms: bounded rationality — incomplete information about the host market; and their non-location-bound FSAs don't transfer as assumed. Add weak localization and non-compliance risk. Lesson: adapt to local culture, invest in real market understanding, and don't assume home advantages travel.
